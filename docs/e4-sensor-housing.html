Sensor Housing Geometry

The printed housing measured from the STL, and the optics it sets up: a TSAL6100 emitter 13.5 mm above the board over an OP505A phototransistor at 7 mm. Four of these, one per sensor, in black resin. This chapter records what the part actually is, the two decisions taken about how to fit it, and what is still unverified.

Two things changed under this chapter. The emitter is now the TSAL6100 (940 nm, ±10°), not the SFH 4550 (850 nm, ±3°) it was designed around — see Ch 3. And the housing in use is the flat variant, not the 5°/7° tilted ones. Those two facts turn out to be connected.

Why flat, and when the tilted bores come back

The 5° and 7° variants exist to cancel that 6.47 mm parallax: tilting the detector up converges the two axes at 6.47 ÷ tan θ — 74 mm at 5° (front-wall range) and 53 mm at 7° (side-wall range). One part for the forward pair, one for the sides. They are a geometric correction, not a detector-aiming nicety.

With the ±3° SFH 4550 that correction was close to mandatory. With the ±10° TSAL6100 it isn't: the cone covers the detector axis across the whole working range, so the flat housing is the right call for the competition build — one part number, no tilted overhang to print, nothing to get handed to the wrong sensor. The tilted STLs stay in reserve. If the bench numbers come back weak specifically on the forward pair at range, the 5° part is the first thing to try, because that is exactly the case it was cut for.

The emitter is located on its flange, not its barrel. A straight ø5.80 bore grips a ø5.8 flange over roughly a millimetre and leaves the ø5.0 barrel floating for the other seven. At 0.1 mm of clearance that is ±4–6° of free tilt, and it is at the wrong end: in the chosen orientation the flange sits at the back and the dome emits at the front, so the whole 8 mm acts as a lever. This is the single largest source of aim scatter in the assembly, and it can't be fixed by pressing harder — only by a two-diameter bore on the next print.

How it mounts

The housing seats flush on that 3.49 × 5.80 mm pad, which fully constrains tilt and elevation — those two angles come from the geometry and no adhesive can drift them. What the pad does not do is hold the part down. Right now the only thing resisting a 16.94 mm tall part rotating on a 20 mm² footprint is four component leads soldered into the board, and thin leads bend plastically: knock it and it moves, and it stays moved.

Bond before you calibrate, not after. The instinct is to glue once the readings look right — but the readings can't be trusted until it's bonded, because the handling that calibration requires (lifting the mouse, squaring it in the cell, doing it again for the next wall configuration) is exactly the loading that shifts an unbonded mount. Every number would come from a slightly different mouse to the one before. Bond first. Hot melt while the aim is still being iterated — rigid enough, releases with heat. Not cyanoacrylate: it blooms a white haze on IR optics as it cures.

Setting the depth

The emitter bore has no stop, so its depth is set entirely by how hard it was pressed. Four housings pressed to four different depths read differently, and that difference then gets written down as four different thresholds — assembly variation wearing a calibration costume. Give it the datum the part lacks:

  1. Press the emitter to flush. Stand the housing tube-tip-down on a sheet of glass or a steel rule and push the LED in from the back until the dome touches. Dome exactly flush with the tube tip, identical on all four to about ±0.1 mm. Press on the flange or barrel — never the dome, which can craze the lens or shift the die.
  2. Let the detector set itself. Its flange stops on the rear face, so depth is fixed for you. Just confirm the flange is fully home.
  3. Index any moulded flat the same way on all four, so package asymmetry is at least consistent between sensors rather than four different aims.
  4. Opaque epoxy at the rear of each bore. Locks the package to the housing, well behind the optics. Note that ordinary clear or amber epoxy is IR-transparent — it holds but does not block. Don't pigment it yourself with graphite or carbon black; both are conductive and the leads are right there.
  5. Aim, then tack. Light one emitter with Emitter hold (BT key E), find the spot with a phone camera, aim at the flat wall face inboard of the slotted post, then fillet the base.
Flush, not recessed. This supersedes the earlier advice to err recessed, which assumed the flat face pointed at the wall. In the stepped-out orientation the 2.22 mm step is the crosstalk defence, and every millimetre the emitter is pushed back spends it. Recessing costs nothing optically — a 10° ray from the dome apex only reaches 0.18 mm off-axis in a millimetre, nowhere near the ø5.80 wall — so this is purely about keeping the step.

Next print

Neither of these is competition-critical — bonded mounts on the existing housings should be enough for trustworthy thresholds — but both are cheap when the part is next cut. Resin holds ±0.05 mm on features this size and prints bores genuinely round, so a press fit is a realistic thing to ask for here in a way it would not be in FDM. Expect a little light-bleed undersize at the bore wall and spec accordingly.

  • Two-diameter bores. Barrel +0.05 for the upper length, with the existing diameter kept as a short relief at the flange end. Takes the emitter journal from ~1 mm to ~4.8 mm and the free tilt from ±4–6° to about ±1°. It also lifts the tube wall from 0.55 to 0.90 mm and the septum from 1.55 to 1.90 — the same change buys the IR path too. Apply it to the detector bore as well if the check above says that one is flange-located.
  • Square the base corners. The seat is 3.49 mm wide only because the corners are rounded off; the part is 6.89. Squaring them doubles the seat area and halves the lean sensitivity, in the azimuth direction, which is the aim axis that matters.

Not yet verified

The housings were printed by an online service in a black resin whose spec isn't known, so IR opacity at 940 nm is an open question — and counterintuitively, black SLA resins are often less near-IR-opaque than they look, because pigment loading is capped by the need to let 405 nm through far enough to cure a layer. Visually jet black and blocking 940 nm through half a millimetre are only loosely related.

Two measurements settle where the bleed actually comes from, and they partition it cleanly. Emitters running, no walls anywhere, and aluminium tape, not black tape — you need something known to be opaque at 940 nm, which is the whole question mark:

  • Foil over the detector's front aperture. Anything still reading is arriving by a route that isn't the front — through the septum, through the tube wall, or around the back.
  • Then foil over the emitter's rear flange too. The drop between the two readings is the rear path specifically. It's a real one: in this orientation both flanges sit on the same rear face, 6.47 mm apart with clear line of sight, and the flange is a disc of clear epoxy that pipes light sideways. The 2.22 mm step fixes the front; it does nothing for the back.

If the first reading comes back near zero, the housing is tight, all the bleed is front-side scatter, and the rear shrouding can be skipped entirely.
