Sensor Geometry & Beam Paths

Where the four IR pairs sit on the mouse, which way each one aims, and how far its beam travels to a wall in a standard 180 mm cell. Origin (0,0) is the mouse's bottom-left corner; the forward direction is +Y.

  • SRDiagonal, 45° forward-right  solder point (52, 90)

Mouse footprint

  • Rectangle 76 mm wide × 62 mm deep, capped by a semicircle of radius 38 mm — overall 100 mm long on the centre line.
  • Centre of rotation (motor axle) at (38, 35). All four sensors sit inside the rounded nose, ~7 mm above the maze floor.
  • Width-centred in the corridor leaves a ~46 mm gap to each side wall (76 mm mouse in a 168 mm clear cell).

Why this matters for the forward-range problem In the worst case all four beams travel essentially the same distance (~100 mm). So if the forward pair reads much weaker than the sides here, distance is not the cause — inverse-square falloff is the same for all of them. That points at optics: the 15° splay plus emitter/detector convergence sending the forward beam's hot-spot where the detector isn't looking. It argues the fix is aim (the 5° housing tilt), not just more current or more settle time.

Read the IR monitor at each and watch the front sum climb: the back-wall figure tells us if the range problem is solved; the centred figure is what thresh_front should be tuned against.

  • One closed square behaves differently from an open maze. With all four walls present and the mouse centred, the 45° diagonals hit the front wall (~41 mm away) before they ever reach a side wall — so a lone test cell won't calibrate the side thresholds the way a real corridor does. The back-wall position keeps each sensor on its intended wall, which is why the diagram uses it.

Standard micromouse cell: 180 mm pitch · 168 mm clear · 12 mm walls · 50 mm tall. Coordinates in mm, mouse frame, origin bottom-left.
