E4 Micromouse · turn geometry

The Turn Table

Sixteen turns, one definition each. Two of them have ever met a floor. This is what each one is for, where it leaves her, which caller will ask for it, and how to tune one.

Nothing past SS90R is driven by anything yet. The solver is four-connected — Heading has four values and the flood walks only N/E/S/W — so there is no diagonal navigation to call these from. They are slots with correct angles and reasoned geometry, waiting for the path generator described in Ch. 9 and an expanded heading set. Their presence in the table is not a feature.

One definition, four readers

The same 90° turn used to be described in three places, and they had already drifted: the menu move hardcoded alpha 1000 while the search carried 2500. So “Right 90” and the turn she made while searching were different turns, and tuning one would not have improved the other.

turn_params[] in mouse.cpp is now the only copy. Four things read it and one of them writes it:

  • The search — turn_smooth() looks up SS90EL / SS90ER each time she turns a corner.
  • The menu 90s — Left 90 and Right 90 run the same rows through control_arc_turn().
  • The tuner — ARC,… writes the selected row, so what you tune is what she runs. No transcription step.
  • The EEPROM — config v5 carries all sixteen, so a tune done at a venue survives a power cycle.

Radius is not a free parameter

You do not set a radius. You set a forward speed and an angular velocity, and the radius falls out of them:

R = v / ω            ω in radians per second

so, for a radius you want, at 300 mm/s:
ω(deg/s) = (300 / R) × 180/π

R = 101 mm  →  ω = 170 deg/s   (the measured search turn)
R =  90 mm  →  ω = 191 deg/s
R =  63 mm  →  ω = 273 deg/s

The other consequence of the radius: for an arc that meets both centrelines cleanly, it has to start about R before the crossing. That is why entry_offset is ~100 for a turn of radius 101 — the two numbers are not independent, and changing ω without moving entry_offset will put her through the wall she was turning to avoid.

“About R” is doing more work in that sentence than it should. R = v/ω is the instantaneous radius once she is rotating at full rate, and at neither end of the turn is she: alpha ramps ω up and back down. Integrating the actual trapezoidal profile at v 300, ω 170, α 2500 gives 111.5 mm forward and 111.5 mm sideways — ten more than R suggests. So entry_offset wants setting from the displacement rather than from R: for this row, about 111, not 100.

A chainable 90 is a 90 mm quarter circle

A turn that runs wall midpoint to wall midpoint — starting where the centreline crosses the wall she enters by, ending where it crosses the wall she leaves by — is exactly a quarter circle of radius 90 mm, half a cell. 90 forward, 90 sideways. None of that is tuneable: it is what the cell pitch is.

It is also the only turn that composes. Because it begins and ends on a cell boundary, on the centreline, the next turn can start exactly where the last one finished. A zigzag becomes arc–arc–arc with no straight between, and two 90s in consecutive cells need precisely the 180 mm the maze gives them.

Her tuned turn swings 111.5 mm, not 90 — 21.5 mm too wide on every turn, so a chained pair needs 223 mm and has 180. For an edge-to-edge turn at 300 mm/s with α 2500 she wants ω ≈ 226 deg/s, which puts the outer wheel at 456 mm/s against 417 today: nine percent more, on a controller ceiling of MAX_MOTOR_VOLTS 4.0 from a 9 V rail.

Only the speed-run row needs this. SS90E and SS90 are separate rows precisely so they can be different turns. Searching, she stops and senses every cell and wall-following re-centres her, so a wide arc costs a little scrub and nothing else — SS90E is tuned and should stay as it is. SS90 is what a fast run strings together, and that is the row that has to fit. Zigzag test drives SS90 by default for exactly that reason, with SS90E selectable as a comparison.

The post loop settles ω without the ramps

From an old lab note: drive into a cell, then round a post continuously, watch the centre drift and adjust α. It is a better rig than the zigzag, and the reason is one line of geometry.

Four chained same-hand 90s of radius 90 ARE a circle centred on a post. She comes up the lane at x = 90 turning right; the arc centre sits 90 mm to her right, which is the post. After the quarter she is heading east and her right is now south — the same point. Every quarter shares a centre, so the four of them are one circle, 565.5 mm round.

Which pins ω exactly: R = v/ω, so R = 90 at 300 mm/s needs ω = 191 deg/s. That is the figure SS180 already holds, for the same reason — its lateral step of 2R also has to be one cell.

191 and 226 are not a contradiction. The 226 above is the figure for a turn driven on its own, where ω ramps to zero at the end and the trapezoid swings wider than the ideal circle. Driven continuously there are no ramps between the quarters, so the ideal R = v/ω applies and 191 is simply right. The two numbers describe two different manoeuvres, and the post loop is the one that matches what a chained turn actually has to be.

And it separates ω from α cleanly. Over a closed lap the four local frames sit at 0, 90, 180 and 270 degrees, so any consistent per-turn displacement error sums to zero — radius, offsets, tangential lag, all of it cancels. Heading does not. At 90 + ε per turn the frames precess, the lap stops closing, and the centre of the circle walks by roughly

error per turndrift per lapafter 8 laps
0.1°0.6 mm5 mm
0.5°3.1 mm25 mm
1.0°6.3 mm50 mm

So ω sets the size of the circle and α sets the drift of its centre. Tune ω until she tracks a 90 mm circle drawn round the post, then α until the centre stops walking. The gyro reports the commanded angle either way, because the loop closes on it — the floor is the only witness, which is the same trap as the gyro scale in Ch 6.

Two things come free. Odometry gives the radius back with no ruler: arc distance over 2π·laps, reported as R_meas beside the 90 it wants. And this is the honest gyro-scale test, at running speed with the wheels rolling rather than scrubbing in place — and the two causes separate, because scale error is speed-independent while α’s contribution grows with ω. Run it with Post loop (Calibration, or A), set up by LOOP,laps,ω,α,hand,speed. It does not write the turn table: 1440 degrees is not a turn. Page 15 of E4-bench-diagnostics-A4.pdf draws the rig.

Above about 400 mm/s the wall is α, not ω

Raising ω buys a tighter turn only while there is room to raise it. With α finite the two ramps carry her sideways on their own, so there is a minimum swing you cannot get below however hard she rotates:

α (°/s²)300 mm/s400500600
25006890113136
500048648096
1000034455668

Minimum achievable lateral swing, mm, for a 90° turn. 90 is what an edge-to-edge turn needs.

At α 2500 an edge-to-edge 90 becomes impossible above about 400 mm/s. No value of ω rescues it — the ramps have already swung her past the boundary. A speed run at 600 mm/s wants SS90 nearer α 10000. So the turn table does not merely need tuning, it needs tuning at the speed you intend to run: a row tuned at 300 does not scale.

Why 45 and 135 each appear twice

A 45° turn is not one turn. This is the single most important thing in the table.

SD45 — straight onto the diagonal planned

Starts on a cell centreline, running square down a column. Ends on a diagonal, running between cell corners.

DS45 — diagonal back to straight planned

The exact reverse. Starts on a diagonal, ends on a cell centreline.

Same angle. Mirrored geometry. Different entry and exit offsets. One row cannot serve both — and a mouse that tries ends up half a cell out on alternate corners only, which is a miserable thing to diagnose because it looks intermittent.

The same split applies at 135°. And DD90 is a third case again: it corners without ever leaving the diagonal, where the pitch between successive corners is 180/√2 = 127 mm rather than 180. That is why its radius is roughly half everything else's.

SS180 is not a dead-end turn. An earlier draft of this table said it was, and that is wrong — geometrically it cannot be. The arc sweeps a full 180 mm of lateral travel, one whole cell pitch, so it needs the neighbouring column to be open. A dead end is by definition walled on both sides and ahead: there is nowhere to swing. Dead ends stay turn_back() — coast to the cell centre, spin in place, drive back out — and the ~3 mm of scrub that costs is the price of the only manoeuvre that fits.

Where SS180 earns its place is the hairpin: a dividing wall with a corridor either side, joined at the far end. She runs up column x, sweeps through the connecting cell and comes back down column x+1 without ever slowing. The path is the one two SS90s would walk; SS180 just refuses to straighten up in the middle of it.

That also makes it a speed-run turn, not a search turn. Committing to the arc means knowing the neighbouring column is open before entering the turn, and during a search she does not know that until she is standing in the cell reading its walls. On a known path the map has already answered the question.

Angles in degrees, positive is left, matching the firmware's sign convention. R in mm, ω in deg/s, both at the 300 mm/s search speed. The two shaded rows are the only ones that have been run against a floor; every other radius is arithmetic.

Entry, exit and lead-out are three different things

This tripped the code up before it tripped anyone else up, so it is worth being explicit.

FieldMeaningWho uses it
entry_offsetWhere the arc starts, as a distance back from the pivot. Should be about R.everyone
exit_offsetA frame relabel applied after the arc. Nothing is driven — the position origin is moved so the next cell lines up.the search only
lead_outA real straight run after the arc.the standalone move and the tuner only

The search drives no lead-out: forward motion simply continues into the next cell. Mapping exit_offset onto lead_out because both sound like “after the turn” would silently change where she thinks she is.

Tuning one

She will always report she turned the commanded angle. The rotation is gyro-closed, so TURNRES telling you gyro=90 means only that the loop closed. The same trap as the gyro scale: the measurement has to come off the floor.

Run it on a maze section rather than open floor — you need the walls to judge against. Start her centred and squared, run one turn, and measure three independent errors:

ErrorHow you see itWhat fixes it
headingNot square to the new corridor.alpha (overshoot), or the gyro scale
lateralNot on the centreline — measure the wall gap on both sides.the radius, via omega
longitudinalToo far along, or not far enough.entry_offset, then lead_out

Fix them in that order, one knob at a time. They are genuinely independent, and changing two together is how an afternoon disappears.

Because ARC writes the live row, the search picks the change up immediately — there is nothing to copy across afterwards. SEL,0–SEL,15 reaches every turn in the table, including the ones nothing drives yet. The app's Tuning screen sends exactly these lines.

The approach is part of the turn

Until 0.33 the tuner could not be asked for one. ARC's fifth field writes entry_offset, and the run passed entry_offset straight in as the lead-in — so the arc always began that many millimetres after she started moving. That is enough to answer does this arc look right. It is no use at all for does she come out of it in the lane, which is the only question a bench can settle.

POS,<cells> counts the approach in cells and lets her derive the millimetres:

entered fromlead-in
a straightBACK_WALL_TO_CENTER + cells × FULL_CELL − entry_offset
the diagonalcells × DIAG_PITCH − entry_offset

DS45, DS135 and DD90 begin on the diagonal. The pitch there is 127.279 mm, not 180, and there is no back wall to square up on — she is placed on the line by hand. Which case applies follows from the row rather than from a switch someone has to remember to set: a turn that leaves the diagonal is a turn that was already on it.

The point of deriving it is that the approach then follows entry_offset instead of being re-typed after every change to it, which is where the arithmetic errors come from. She reports TUNE,pos=,lead=,pitch= so what she will drive is visible before anything moves, and TURNRES now carries the row and the lead — three runs back you will not remember which row was selected.

The arithmetic before the floor

An arc of radius R joining two lanes that cross at angle θ is tangent to both only if it begins R·tan(θ/2) before the crossing and ends the same distance after. That is a fact about circles. Combined with R = v / ω from earlier in this chapter, every row in the table makes a claim that can be checked without moving.

With one wrinkle, and it took drawing the maze out 1:1 to see it. entry_offset is measured back from the LATTICE POINT the planner books the turn at. For most turns that point is also where the two lanes cross, so the tangent is the whole story. An SD45 is booked in at a CELL CENTRE — but a straight lane meets a diagonal at the WALL MIDPOINT, half a cell earlier, because the diagonal enters the cell through its side wall. She has to be turning by then. So SD45 and SD135 carry 90 mm on top of the tangent, and nothing else does:

The two 45 rows cannot both be right while they share a number. SD45 needs 165 and DS45 needs 75; both hold 120. An arc that is not tangent to either lane misses both, and every measurement you take afterwards is measuring that rather than the thing you changed.

The same 120 sits in the planner as sd45_offset and ds45_offset (Ch. 10), so those move with them. E4-bench-diagnostics-A4.pdf draws all of this at 1:1 — the centrelines, the corner-to-corner lines, and the diamond joining the four wall midpoints which is the diagonal lane itself. The half cell is obvious the moment you see the diamond: it touches the wall midpoints and misses the cell centre entirely. And one number makes the consequence concrete: a diagonal step is 127.279 mm, of which ds45_offset is 120 — so a DS45 entered one step after an SD45 has seven millimetres of approach before its arc starts. At the tangent value it has 52. Below 63.6 mm each, both tangents fit inside one step; above it, that geometry does not exist and the planner is right to refuse it.

Rigs, one per turn

A turn buried in the middle of a competition maze cannot be profiled: by the time she reaches it she is carrying whatever error the turns before it left behind. So each turn gets a maze of its own, small enough to build out of one 3×3 bench section — mazefiles/bench/, twelve of them, each a single corridor so there is exactly one route through it and nothing else to watch.

The corridors were not chosen by eye. tools/bench-mazes/bench.cpp enumerates every single-corridor maze that fits in a section from every start pose it allows — 644 in a 3×3 — plans each with the real planner, and reports which rig shows each turn best. Two results came out of that:

  • The competition corner can only turn right. At (0,0) facing north the west perimeter is beside her, so her first turn in a 3×3 is a right whatever the walls say — and SD45_L is not reachable there at all without a spin in the route.
  • (2,0) facing north is the mirror image of (0,0) facing north, and mirroring is exactly what swaps left for right. These are tuning rigs, not competition runs, so she is placed by hand — and once the start pose is allowed to move, every left-hand rig is the x-mirror of its right-hand twin, walk for walk, identical to the millisecond in the model.

tools/bench-mazes/checktxt reads the shipped ASCII back — walls, goal and all — and derives the start pose from the file itself: a single corridor has two ends, one is the goal, and the other is where she gets put down, heading along its one opening. So the pose is verified rather than trusted.

What has to exist before the diagonals do

This list was written when none of it existed. Three of the four have since landed, and the original shape is worth keeping so the one that has not is not quietly forgotten.

  • An expanded heading set. done Not by widening Heading, which stayed four cardinal values. native.cpp carries a second enum, Diag, over a half-cell lattice — and the two being different enums offset by half a turn is exactly what broke the executor in 0.29. They now meet in one place, diag_ccw() / diag_cw() in native.h.
  • The path generator. done Ch. 9 and Ch. 10. Dial's over the lattice, emitting the move vocabulary directly rather than run-length-encoding a cell list after the fact.
  • Multi-cell straights. done run_route() drives straights of any length, and gave up the per-cell re-anchoring to do it — which is what makes the last item the one that now matters.
  • Working side sensors. still open The only thing left in the way. Diagonal running has no walls either side at the usual distance, so the centring reference changes entirely. Nothing above is worth trusting at speed until the thresholds in Ch. 6 are real.
