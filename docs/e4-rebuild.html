The E4 Rebuild

A classic micromouse, rebuilt from the firmware up — a mazerunner-core brain on an STM32F411, an IR wall-sensing front end aimed by hand, and a Web Bluetooth companion that watches the mouse think. These are the notes from getting it running.

The brain

A search that maps both ways

At the core is a port of mazerunner-core — the flood-fill maze solver — onto the STM32F411's mixed C/C++ world, ticking inside a 1 kHz control loop. The mouse searches out to the goal, then keeps mapping on the way back to the start, so a single run learns the maze in both directions before it commits to a fast route.

Two rules give it competition manners. A centre-room "one entrance" fill keeps the mouse from wandering the 2×2 goal box looking for a second door, and once the search closes it reports the optimal known route — the shortest path the accumulated map can prove — rather than just retracing its footprints. Every wall it learns streams out over Bluetooth as it goes, so the map builds up live on the companion app.

The eyes

Trust the data, not the description

Wall sensing is four IR emitter/detector pairs, read with ambient subtraction: sample dark, pulse the emitter for a few microseconds, sample lit, subtract. That one trick rejects daylight and room IR, so a wall reads the same on the bench as under competition lights. The four sit on the front dome — the forward pair (FL/FR) on the outer edges, the diagonal pair (SL/SR) inboard and a little further forward.

The interesting part was which sensor watches what. The verbal wiring notes said one thing; the raw ADC counts said the opposite. With a wall on the left only, the inner-left sensor read 112 and the outer-left read 7 — so the inner diagonal pair (SL/SR) watches the side walls and the outer pair (FL/FR) looks forward, opposite to the first guess. We took the data's word for it, and the wall reads jumped from single digits in open space to the 300–500 range up close.

The link

A companion that watches it think

Over the HM-18 BLE module, the mouse streams a compact telemetry protocol — pose, walls, motion state, sensor values — into a browser app built on Web Bluetooth. The maze redraws as she explores, the live sensor panel lights up over a photo of the actual chassis, and a battery gauge reads straight off the stream. No install, just a page.

The app also sends mazes back to the mouse for simulation, and that exposed a bug: the blue trail was crossing amber walls. The maze was arriving corrupted a byte at a time over the air. The fix was to stop trusting the link — every injected row now carries a checksum, retries up to ten times, and is read back and verified before the next one goes. Belt, braces, and a receipt.

The datasheet said index 3 for 57 600 baud. The module wanted index 6.

HM-18 clones map AT+BAUD linearly, not by the published table. Once the index matched, the 230 400 → 57 600 change took, and the link stopped dropping bytes under load.

The memory

A map that survives the power switch

A 24LC256 EEPROM on the I2C bus holds the discovered maze — a small header (magic E4M1, the goal, 256 wall bytes, a checksum), written after each search. Power-cycle the mouse, hit recall, and the map comes straight back. Which made the failure baffling: every write "succeeded," every read came back 0xFF.

The chip was fine. The driver was fine. The bus was fine. Buzzing out the joints found it — the EEPROM's ground pin was isolated on the board, floating where the schematic showed it grounded. A single jumper wire to ground, and the round-trip passed first try.

The chip wasn't faulty. Its ground was never connected.

Every diagnostic pointed at silicon — present on the bus, writes acknowledging, reads flat. The real fault was a floating Vss pin. A hardware problem wearing a firmware costume.

It also drew a clean line between two mazes that are easy to conflate: the discovered map the mouse remembers, which lives in EEPROM, and the ground-truth maze the simulator senses against, which lives only in RAM. Recall replays the first; a simulation needs the second re-loaded. In the real world that distinction dissolves — the physical walls never go anywhere, so the saved map is just a head start toward a stand-and-deliver speed run.

The power

How long does it actually run?

The pack is a 1S 320 mAh LiPo; the motors are Faulhaber 1516T009SR coreless — 32.5 Ω windings, rated for 9 V. And there's the twist: that 9 V motor rail is boosted up from the 3.7 V cell. A switching converter transforms power, not current, so the battery doesn't see the motor current — it sees the motor power reflected back, stepped up by roughly the voltage ratio. Hard acceleration that pulls a modest current at the motor lands as a much heavier draw on the little cell.

Knowing the shape of the draw, the mouse now protects its own pack. A firmware guard samples the battery inside the motion loops and, if the voltage stays below 3.30 V for 200 ms, parks the motors and flags it — sampled between the bursts so it trips on genuine depletion, not a momentary sag. The app mirrors it: a live gauge, a percentage off the LiPo curve, and a low-water mark that catches the deepest dip of a run even after the voltage springs back.

What's next

Two modes, and the road to fast

The menu is splitting into a Test / Bench world for calibration, simulation and diagnostics, and a Competition world for the run modes — wall follower and full solver, with search speed, integrated turns and diagonal navigation as options. The realisation underneath: "integrated turns" is one arc primitive that serves both a flowing wall follower and a faster search, and diagonals are the capstone that needs it. That build is tracked, slot by slot, on the companion E4 Build Map.
