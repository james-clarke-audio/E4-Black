E4 Micromouse · sensor mechanics

The Sensor Carrier

One printed plate that holds all four of the existing sensor housings at computed positions and angles. It exists because the aim was never the problem — the positions were. This is what the board file said, what the two datasheets forced, and the four things that had to be caught in a slicer before anything was printed.

This is for the second board, not the one that runs today. The mouse on the bench keeps its hand-aimed sensors and still gets calibrated as it stands — Ch. 3 and Ch. 4 describe that build and remain correct for it. The plate goes on a spare, already-populated board, so nothing has to be desoldered.

What the board actually does

Plotted to scale from documents/schematics/E4-blackpill-v2.brd, with the mouse centred in a cell, the side beam does not land where the chapter on aim says it should.

IR-SIDELEFT sits at (24.77, 88.90) rotated 60°. That is 24.8 mm inboard of the board edge, which puts it 70.3 mm from the side wall — so at 60° the beam has to travel 81.1 mm to reach it, the far end of the 40–90 mm useful range. It arrives at y = 129.5.

The forward post spans y 119–131. So the beam lands on the post — 4.5 mm past its centre, 1.5 mm from its trailing edge. That is not a weak reading, it is a reading taken at the seam between this cell's wall and the next one's. Where the next cell has an opening on that side, the beam passes straight through the gap and the channel reports no wall while there is a wall beside her. It reads the wrong cell.

The angle was not the problem

The instinct is to open the bore angle. Solving position and angle together says otherwise. Even at the board's existing 60°, moving the optic outboard still lands on the post; 65° catches its edge. 70° is the shallowest angle that reaches flat wall at all:

More oblique buys signal and spends look-ahead. Left at 70° because that is the first row that clears the post, keeping as much forward view as the geometry allows. It is one line in the generator if it wants to move.

What does the work is moving the optic outboard, not opening the angle. That is the whole argument for a plate: a housing bonded straight to the board can only sit where its pads are, while a plate holds it wherever the CAD says and lets the leads take up the difference.

Why the existing housing, and not a new carrier

The first four attempts replaced the housing entirely, holding each part in a printed 90° V-trough — the reasoning being that a 0.4 mm nozzle cannot hold a ø5.8 mm bore to optical tolerance, while a V locates a cylinder whatever the print came out at.

That reasoning is still sound. The design was not. It kept failing on things a solid-model check does not look at, and every one of them was caught by eye in a slicer preview:

  • Every optic was blind. The trough cut started at the dome tip but the block face stood 0.5 mm in front of it, so all eight beams fired into a wall of PLA. Visible in the slicer as a thin flange across each part.
  • No lead holes. All sixteen pads were covered by the carrier. Nothing could reach the board.
  • The beam cone, not the axis. After both of those were fixed, the axis was clear for all eight — but 14 of 32 cone rays on FL and 6 on FR were still blocked. The tests had been sampling the centre line only.
  • The plate sat through the Black Pill. Its real library footprint is 20.32 × 52.07, offset and rotated 90°; the clearance check had been using a guessed, centred box. Every version up to that point intersected it.
The common thread is checking a part as a solid instead of as an optical mount. Watertight, one body, overhang angles, wall thickness, volume — all green, on a part that could not see, could not be wired, and occupied the same space as the MCU. The verification that matters here traces rays and lead paths against the plate and the parts standing in it, and that is what the generator now does on every build.

With the housings already printed and proven, reusing them removes the whole class of problem: the optics sit where they already sit relative to each other, the leads reach the pads the way they already do on the bench mouse, and the plate has one job left — position and azimuth.

That vertical stack is what makes the lead problem tractable. The TSAL6100 has 26.5 mm of lead and the OP505A only 12.70 mm; the detector sits 5.7 mm lower precisely where the shorter lead needs it to be.

Emitter over the plate, detector along a trench

The plate is 6.4 mm thick, from z = 2.0 to 8.4. The two optics are on opposite sides of that top face, and they need different relief.

  • The emitter, at 12.743, looks out over the plate. Its cone (half-angle + 5° of margin) is subtracted for clearance and that is all it needs.
  • The detector, at 7.021, is below the plate top. Give it a cone and it peers down a conical tunnel with a restricted field of view — which is exactly what a first attempt did, leaving a visible cone-shaped hole in the plate in front of each detector. It gets an open trench instead: everything above z = 4.0 in front of it is removed, so it looks out over open sky.
The trench floor turns out to be useful. It blocks the detector's downward periphery — rays more than about 20° below axis — while leaving everything level and upward clear out to 35°. Downward is the floor-bounce direction (Ch. 3), so that is a baffle rather than an obstruction. And the emitter's own beam does not descend to z = 4 until roughly 50 mm out, well past the plate, so the trench floor is never illuminated.

Thickness, and the emitter shroud

The plate started at 3 mm and was too thin to trust. Measured rather than eyeballed: the narrowest neck — where the front arc meets the rear anchors — was 0.8 mm across, two extrusion widths at a 0.4 mm nozzle, and the section at mid-height was in 37 disconnected fragments.

The fix comes from the housing itself. The emitter shroud's underside sits at 9.296 mm, and those necks run directly beneath it — so the plate can be 6.4 mm thick and still pass under. Necks to 2.4 mm, fragments to 3, and the pocket now grips each housing over 6.4 mm of its height rather than 3.

Enlarging the pocket collars to gain more was tried and made it worse — 0.8 mm at a 10 mm collar, because a larger collar creates fresh thin features where it meets its neighbours. Left at 2.4 mm, which is six perimeters.

Holding it down — and getting it wrong twice

The board carries five 2 mm holes. Four of them are spoken for twice over — the motor mounts and the U$1 daughterboard both declare holes at (17, 17), (60, 17), (60, 51) and (17, 51) — which left only the nose apex at (38.5, 98), and one hole is a pivot.

The first printed version therefore gripped the board's side edges at y = 58, on the reasoning that if the holes were unavailable the rim was the next best anchor. That version was printed and it failed, for a reason no amount of checking the plate against itself would ever have found.

The grippers were in the wheel wells. The board is not a rectangle with a semicircular nose. Between y = 14.5 and y = 54.5 it steps in to x = 11.46 and 65.54 to clear the wheels, and only flares back out above 54.5. The grippers at x = 1.0 and 76.0 sat about 8 mm inside those wells and fouled the tyres. The plate was checked against the real outline; the feet were placed against a remembered one.

The board slot was also cut 1.8 mm for a 1.6 mm PCB and closed up in the print. Vertical pockets come out near-nominal in FDM; a horizontal slot loses 0.2–0.4 mm to squish and sag and wants +0.6. Moot now — there is no slot in the current part — but the asymmetry is worth carrying forward.

The foot is the motor-mount rail

The way out was in the silkscreen the whole time. Each motor mount is a 40 × 6 × 15 mm block standing on the board — that is the plain white outline on layer 21, the rail at x 14–20 running y 14.5–54.5, mirrored at x 57–63 — and it stands on one M2 nut per stud, purely as a spacer.

So the plate's foot does not need to find somewhere new to sit. It replaces that nut: a 6 × 40 × 1.6 mm tongue lying inside the white outline with both studs passing through it, and the mount slots down on top exactly as before. A nut is about 4 mm across; the foot bears over 240 mm². Whatever the mount's base profile turns out to be in detail, more of it is supported than was before.

That makes five fixings — four studs and the apex — where the first version had two hooks and a pivot, and nothing anywhere near a wheel.

1.6 mm, not 2. The obvious instinct is to make a printed foot thicker than the nut it replaces. It is the wrong instinct, because the wheels are the datum. The axle height above the floor is fixed by the wheel; the mount is fixed to the axle. Packing the mount up off the board does not raise the mount — it lowers the board, taking the sensors and the ground clearance down with it. A 2 mm foot would have dropped the optics from 7.0 to 6.6 mm. At exactly one nut thickness, nothing on the mouse moves.

Between the foot and the nose the plate has to get from x ≈ 17 out to the board edge and past the Black Pill, which occupies x 12.7–64.8 from y = 57.2 forward. It stays at foot height for the mount's full length, steps up to the 6.4 mm plate at y = 54.8 — 0.3 mm past the mount's end — and threads the corridor between the board edge and the Black Pill. Forward of y = 57 it hangs 0.8 mm past the board edge, which is free air there, and that is what widens the pinch from 1.8 to 3.0 mm.

The apex boss now runs the full way to the board rather than stopping at the plate underside. On the gripper version the hooks set the standoff and the apex bolt was only a locator; with the hooks gone it was left spanning 2 mm of air, and tightening it would have bowed the nose down.

It is not being printed in PLA

The production part is MJF in PA12 nylon, dyed black — HP Multi Jet Fusion, a powder bed, not FDM. Three things about this part were reasoned in the wrong material, and one of them is worth stopping for.

The pocket clearance had to be opened. FIT was 0.30 mm on both axes, chosen for FDM. MJF holds ±0.3% with a lower limit of ±0.3 mm, and asks for at least 0.6 mm between parts that have to assemble. At 0.30 nominal the worst case is zero — a pocket a housing will not enter without filing, four times over, after a three-day turnaround. It is now 0.50 mm.

Opening it is not free: the pocket is what fixes the azimuth. Slack c across a pocket that grips over the housing's 8.025 mm depth allows about atan(c/8.025) of rotation — 2.1° at 0.30, 3.6° at 0.50, 4.3° at 0.60. At 70° on the sides that is real: 65° already catches the post's edge.

Which is survivable only because the angle is set at glue-up, not by the pocket. The pocket's job is to hold the housing roughly right and square while the epoxy goes off; the aim comes from sighting the emitter on a phone camera and peaking the detector on a live reading. Read that way the pocket is a fixture, not a datum, and 0.50 mm is the right trade — worst case 0.2 and assemblable, best case 0.8 and held by the epoxy. It is the tight end that has no recovery.

The other two are smaller. Orientation and supports are not ours to choose — a powder bed needs neither, so the 0.0 mm² overhang figure and the “feet down on the bed” note apply to a home FDM test print and to nothing else. And PA12 is about half as stiff as PLA (1750 MPa against roughly 3500) at similar strength and far greater toughness, so the 3.0 × 6.4 mm arm will deflect around twice as far under the same load — but it will bend and come back rather than snap, which for a part carrying the optics past a wall impact is the better trade. Five bolted fixings and short arms keep the deflection small.

Worth a torch test when it arrives. MJF PA12 comes off the machine mid-grey — L* ≈ 55 — and the black is a dye, not pigment through the bulk. A dye chosen to look black in daylight need not absorb anything at 940 nm. 6.4 mm of nylon will attenuate a great deal regardless, and the housings do the near-field separation, but the trench floor is described above as a baffle and that claim is untested in this material. Hold the part between an emitter and a phone camera — phone sensors see 940 nm — and see whether it glows.

Everything else clears the process comfortably: minimum wall 1 mm against a 1.6 mm foot and 3.0 mm arm, minimum hole 2 mm against 2.3 and 2.5 mm clearance holes, minimum detail 0.25 mm, layer 0.08 mm. No enclosed voids, so no powder to trap.

Printing and fitting

  • MJF: nothing to specify. Powder bed — no orientation choice, no supports, no perimeters.
  • FDM test print: feet down, flat on the bed; the plate body's 2 mm underside is standoff over the SMDs, not material. Supports none — genuine overhang is 0.0 mm². At least four perimeters: black PLA blocks 940 nm well, but FDM light-pipes along layer boundaries.
  1. Take the four nuts out from under the motor mounts and lift the mounts off the studs.
  2. Lay the plate on, feet over the studs, apex M2 at (38.5, 98) with a nut under the board.
  3. Put the mounts back straight onto the feet, then the two nuts and the daughterboard above each as before.
  4. Drop each housing through its pocket until it seats on the PCB. The pockets are U-channels: both flanks run the full 8 mm, which is what fixes the azimuth; front and rear are open for the beam and the leads.
  5. Check the azimuth, then epoxy in the pocket.
  6. Fit the parts to the housings and form the leads as on the current mouse. Bend right at the rear face and trim only after forming — the detector has almost no lead to spare.
Do not lean on the driver. The foot is a printed part taking the mount's clamp load where a steel nut used to. M2 at sensible torque is fine; if it crushes, the axles dip and the sensor height goes with them.

What this does not settle

  • The current part has not been printed. The gripper version was, and four separate defects came out of it — every one spotted by eye in the slicer, none by the verification above. Treat the table as necessary and nowhere near sufficient: it proves the plate does not block itself, not that the plate fits the mouse.
  • The free tilt inside the housing bores is unchanged. The plate fixes the housing's angle, not the part's angle within it — and by Ch. 5 that is still ±4–6° for the emitter and about ±8° for the detector. This is now the dominant error in the assembly.
  • The return model is approximate. A wall is an extended surface rather than a point, so the round trip is nearer 1/d² than 1/d⁴; the figure quoted is the conservative one.
  • The target is one position. y = 112.9 is right with the mouse centred in a cell. She reads walls at SENSING_POSITION, well forward of that, and the landing point moves with her.
  • The thresholds will need re-measuring for this board — on a maze floor section, not a bench top.
