E4 Micromouse · route planning

Planning the Fast Run

The flood in maze.h answers fewest cells. A fast run needs least time, and those are different questions on a different graph. This is that graph, the two ways to put diagonals on it, and the validator that caught both of them lying.

Fewest cells is not least time

The classic flood assigns every cell a cost of one and floods outward from the goal. It is the right tool for the search run: cheap, re-run every cell, and robust to the map changing underneath it. What it cannot express is that some cells are quicker to cross than others — because in a unit-cost flood, cost is a property of the cell.

Time is not a property of a cell. She accelerates. Four separate one-cell moves and one four-cell straight are not the same journey, and no number written into m_cost[x][y] can say so. The cost of crossing a cell depends on how fast she is going when she reaches it, which depends on how far away the next turn is — information the cell does not have.

So a per-cell weighting cannot be rescued by choosing better weights. The graph has to change shape.

What restores additivity

The turn table does, and this is the whole reason the planner is tractable. Every turn in turn_params runs at a constant forward speed. So a straight between two turns has a known entry speed and a known exit speed, and its duration is a closed form of (length, v_in, v_out, v_max, accel): accelerate, maybe cruise, brake to meet the next turn. Segment costs stop depending on each other, which is exactly the condition a shortest-path search needs.

That gives the graph its shape:

the floodthe planner
nodea cella turn — (cell, outgoing heading, speed class)
edgeone cell steprun straight N cells, then take the next turn
cost1straight time + turn time
count2562048

Nodes exist only where she changes direction. A six-cell straight is one edge, which is precisely what lets the cost account for acceleration. And every turn offers two implementations — a smooth arc at the table speed, or stop and spin — with the search choosing. That matters twice over: it is how a tight section correctly comes out as stop-and-spin, and it is what keeps the planner honest when an arc cannot be entered in the space available.

The queue had to go too. The old flood used a plain FIFO, which is correct only while every edge costs the same — that is what unit cost bought, and why an Atmel could get away with it. With real times the edges differ and a FIFO gives wrong answers. It is Dial’s algorithm now: a circular bucket queue, still O(E) because the weights are small bounded integers, with a guard that invalidates the plan rather than clamping if an edge ever outgrows the ring.

Two rules of the competition, worth real time

A run is timed from the moment she leaves the start square to the moment she enters the target. Both ends of that are worth something.

  • The run-up inside the start square is free. The clock has not started, so what crosses the line is whatever speed she has built in the 139 mm from the back wall — about 745 mm/s at 2000 mm/s², rather than nothing.
  • She never has to stop in the goal. The clock stops on entry. She only has to be able to stop within the run-out the goal box offers, roughly 270 mm, which caps entry at about 1040 mm/s. That is a constraint on the route, not a cost in it.

At her current numbers there is nothing to find

The first thing the bench reported was that the whole exercise is worth little as she stands. SEARCH_SPEED is 300 mm/s and SEARCH_TURN_SPEED is 300 mm/s. She travels exactly as fast down a straight as she does round a corner. The only lever left is turn count, and SHORTEST’s tie-break already prefers fewer spins — so the two answers collapse onto each other on most mazes and the weighted planner is an expensive way to reproduce the flood.

An earlier version of this page said more than that — that with equal speeds “every route of equal length takes equal time and there is no quickest to find”. That is not true, and the bench says so: a turn costs time whatever forward speed she holds through it, so two routes of the same cell count but different turn counts do not tie. At v_max 300 on tricky.maz the two objectives already separate, 67.830 s against 66.100 s. Equal speeds make the gap small; they do not make it zero.

Diagonals, two ways

A diagonal is worth having because it turns a staircase of 90° turns into one straight line at 45°, and because the pitch along it is 180/√2 = 127.28 mm rather than 180. There are two ways to get one, and they are not equivalent.

  • Classic substitution. Plan the route on cells as if diagonals did not exist, then find the zigzags and replace each with SD45 onto the diagonal, a diagonal run, DS45 back off. Cheap, and it is what most mice have always done.
  • Native search. Put the diagonal moves into the graph and search. Slower to compute, and it can do the one thing substitution cannot: choose a longer cell route because that route diagonalises better. Post-processing can only improve the route it was handed, and the orthogonal planner threw the good one away before it ever ran.

The lattice

Work in half-cells — u = x/90, v = y/90, both 0–32 — and parity alone says what a point is:

paritywhat it iswhere
odd, oddcell centrecell (x,y) is at (2x+1, 2y+1)
even, oddvertical-wall midpointthe diagonal runs
odd, evenhorizontal-wall midpointthrough these
even, evena POST — never occupied—

A diagonal is a straight line through wall midpoints, stepping (±1,±1), so the parity alternates between the two wall kinds and it never lands on a post.

It is not the line joining cell centres diagonally. That line passes straight through a post. Running NE out of cell (0,1) she joins y = x + 90 — through (180,270), (270,360), (360,450) — wall midpoints, one step of 127.28 mm apart, clear of every post. The SD45 does not merely turn her 45°; it also shifts her about 64 mm sideways off the centreline onto that line.

The two transitions are the same offset in each direction, which is the only tidy thing about the geometry:

  • SD45 takes a centre at (2x+1, 2y+1) to the wall midpoint at centre + (d − h)
  • DS45 takes a wall midpoint (u,v) to the centre at (u,v) + h

where h is the orthogonal heading and d the diagonal one, so (d − h) is the unit orthogonal step 45° from h toward d. Heading N and turning onto NE, (d − h) is E: she joins the diagonal at the east wall of the cell she is in. And a diagonal step crosses one cell corner to corner, so it needs the wall it enters by and the wall it leaves by to both be openings of that cell.

Corner to corner

A diagonal step does not run down a corridor. It cuts across a cell, entering at the midpoint of one wall and leaving at the midpoint of an adjacent one — never the opposite one, because opposite walls would be a straight. So the rule for whether a step is legal is not about the walls around a corridor, it is about one cell: both the wall she enters by and the wall she leaves by must be openings of the same cell, and that cell is the one containing the midpoint of the step.

In half-cells the arithmetic is cx = (u + u₂) / 4, cy = (v + v₂) / 4. One wall coordinate is always even and the other odd, so the sum is odd and the division floors correctly whichever way she is travelling.

Getting that one line wrong is how the search silently did nothing. The first version computed the cell as (u + u₂ − 1) / 2, which lands on the neighbour. Every diagonal step then checked the wrong cell’s walls, found itself blocked almost everywhere, and the search quietly returned routes identical to the orthogonal ones. It did not crash and it did not report an error — it produced a plausible answer with the feature switched off. Only comparing it against classic substitution, which it should have beaten by construction, showed that anything was wrong.

What she is threading

Corner to corner means she passes between posts rather than between walls, and that is a much tighter proposition than it sounds.

One diagonal step across one cell. Green: the two wall midpoints. Red: the three posts that bound the corridor — one on the inside of the turn, two on the outside. Dashed: the free corridor.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. The diagonal passes 90/√2 = 63.6 mm from each of those three post centres. A post is 12 mm square, so its nearest corner sits 6√2 = 8.5 mm closer. That leaves 55.2 mm of free half-width, and a corridor 110.3 mm wide — against 168 mm for a straight one.

straight corridordiagonal corridor
clear width168.0 mm110.3 mm
board alone, 77 mm45.5 mm a side16.7 mm a side
with the sensor plate and housings, 81.6 mm43.2 mm a side14.4 mm a side

A third of the clearance. That single number is most of why diagonals are the hard part, and it is worth holding next to Ch 11: the sensor plate and the side housings stand about 2.3 mm proud of the board rim on each side, which costs nothing in a straight and is a sixth of the remaining margin on a diagonal.

It also changes what the side sensors are looking at. In a corridor they see a continuous wall and steering has something to lock to. On a diagonal they see posts — a periodic return every 127 mm with nothing in between — so diagonal steering is a different problem from wall-following, not a tuning of it. Nothing in this chapter addresses that; the planner assumes she can hold the line, and holding the line is the part that has not been built.

What the validator found

The two methods disagreed, and neither could adjudicate — a planner that scores its own output is only ever as honest as its own model. So route_check() walks a route over the lattice against the map, independently of whichever planner produced it, and returns the first step that is not drivable.

It immediately found that classic substitution was reporting times for routes she could not drive. Two bugs, neither of them visible to any amount of timing arithmetic:

1 — only one of the two 45s is ever legal. Coming off the diagonal the landing point is (u,v) + h, and it has to be a cell centre. At a vertical-wall midpoint she is on a row centreline but straddling a wall line, so only E or W are available; at a horizontal one, only N or S. The other lands on a post. Classic chose the exit hand by which way the last turn of the zigzag went, which is unrelated to the lattice.
2 — the diagonal does not end where the zigzag ended. A k-turn zigzag finishes its last turn in a cell; the diagonal comes off a cell or so further along. Splice it in without noticing and the straight after it is counted from the wrong place — she overshoots by exactly one cell, and every wall check from there on is against the wrong cell.

Fixing both, and costing the join with the corrected length, took classic from a claimed 8–10% to a real 3.4%. The earlier figure is in the repository history and in an earlier version of the README; it was wrong, and it was wrong in the direction that flatters the work.

Watching it on the app

Plan route — Maze solver, or P — runs the planner three ways over the map she is currently holding and streams each answer to the companion apps, which draw all three over the same maze. Shortest dashed, quickest amber, diagonal green on top. No motors are involved, so it works on an injected maze, one recalled from EEPROM, or one she has just finished exploring: a whole run can be rehearsed at the bench before she drives a cell of it.

message
RT,<kind>,<ms>start of a route — kind 0 shortest, 1 quickest, 2 diagonal; ms is the predicted time, or −1 for no route
RP,<u>,<v>,<move>a vertex, in half-cells, with the move made there
RTE,<pts>,<cells>,<turns>,<spins>end

Half-cells are the reason this was a small change on the app side. They are the unit the lattice already works in, and the parity that makes them useful in the planner makes them useful on the wire too: a cell centre is an odd pair, a wall midpoint is a mixed one, and a diagonal leg is just a line from one point to the next. Neither companion has to know about the lattice, the parity rule, or which wall a point sits on — draw a polyline through the points and it is right, orthogonal and 45° alike. Sending cells instead would have meant a new message for diagonals and geometry in two apps.

It reads the live turn table. The planner is handed turn_params as it stands, not a compiled-in copy, so what it costs a turn at is whatever the tuner last wrote. Plan, change a row, plan again, and watch the route move — which is the quickest way to see whether a turn is worth tuning before spending an evening on it.

The firmware carries only native.cpp. With diagonals disabled it is the orthogonal planner, and tools/planner-bench/xcheck confirms the two agree in shape on all 392 mazes, so planner.cpp stays out of the build and the working set is about 46 KB rather than 82. planner.cpp remains in the repository as the simpler reference the bench cross-checks against.

The search speed was the wrong speed

When Plan route first ran on the mouse it returned 27.245 s for shortest and 27.245 s for quickest, with byte-identical point lists. The cause was not subtle and it was mine: act_plan_route built its Robot from SEARCH_SPEED and SEARCH_ACCELERATION, so it was planning the fast run at search speed.

They are not the same number and should never have shared one. Search speed is bounded by sensing — she reads walls and decides where to go once per cell, so how fast she may explore is a sensor question settled at the bench. The fast run has no such limit, because the map is already known. 0.22 splits them:

constantdefaultwhy
RUN_SPEED600 mm/stwice the search speed — a deliberately timid opening bid
RUN_ACCELERATION2000 mm/s²as the search, until it is measured
RUN_DIAG_SPEED300 mm/s110.3 mm between posts against 168 down a cell. She threads it

The arc speeds are untouched and stay as the turn table holds them. They are independent: she can run a six-cell straight flat out and still take every SS90 at the speed its row was tuned at, and that independence is the entire mechanism by which a longer, straighter line starts to beat a shorter, twistier one.

Sweeping the straight speed across the full competition mazes then says something more useful than the split itself — it says where her lap time actually lives:

300 → 600 buys 23%. 600 → 1500 — two and a half times the straight speed — buys another 10%. These routes are turn-dominated: sixteen arcs at 300 mm/s swamp anything the straights can save. Past about 1000 mm/s RUN_SPEED is close to free money that is not there, and the turn table is worth more bench time than the straight-line number.

Runtime rather than const, like the turn table and the spin dynamics — SPD? and SPD,v,a,diag over BT, persisted as config block v6. Nothing drives on them yet, so a wrong value costs an estimate and not a mouse.

How much of the route is still guesswork

“Have I explored enough?” had no answer but a feeling. It has one now, and it is a proof rather than a percentage — which matters, because a percentage here would have been invented and this is not.

act_plan_route already flips the wall mask, so the second plan is nearly free. Run the same search twice with opposite assumptions:

  • MASK_CLOSED — an unseen wall is a wall. The best route she can prove.
  • MASK_OPEN — an unseen wall is an opening. The best route that could possibly exist.

The second is a true lower bound, because the real maze has at least as many walls as the optimistic view of it and adding walls can never make a route faster. So the gap between them is the most that is still out there to find, and when it closes her route is not probably the best, it is the best and no further exploring can change that.

And it sharpens “what is left to explore” into something much smaller. The cells worth driving to are exactly the not-yet-fully-seen cells on the optimistic route. An unknown cell that no optimistic route passes through cannot change the answer, however blank it looks on the screen.

The case that makes this concrete is a long corridor along the bottom of the map. Driving the row above it, she reads its north walls and learns nothing at all of its east–west state, so every cell in it is “unvisited” by cell_is_visited(), which is true only when all four walls have been seen. Whether any of that matters is not a judgement call: it is decided by whether the best possible line wants to go down there. If it does not, those cells are dead ground and she should never spend a second on them.

On the wire, alongside the three RT/RP/RTE answers:

RB,<kind>,<ms>,<unknown cells on that route>
RU,<x>,<y>  …  RUE,<total>        the union — and only these matter

The union is taken across all three kinds deliberately. It is a superset of what an orthogonal-only run needs, which is the safe direction to be wrong in while the diagonal arcs are untuned. The OLED shows known, best possible and the unseen count, and reads PROVED when there is nothing left; both apps draw the unseen cells as dashed amber squares under the route lines, so the count and the place agree.

route_cells() is not route_points(). Points are the corners you would draw; cells are the ground she covers, and the two sets differ. A six-cell straight is two points and six cells. A diagonal step is no point at all, but it does cross one cell corner to corner — and the cell it crosses is (u+u2)>>2, not (u+u2-1)>>1, which lands on the neighbour. That arithmetic cost an evening once, so the new walker shares diag_step()’s expression rather than deriving its own.

Cost is one extra plan per kind, about 100 ms each on the F411, so roughly 600 ms added to a press of P.

What this does not settle

  • Nothing drives any of it. Plan route runs the planner and draws the answer, but no run mode executes one. The gap between a route on the screen and a route under the wheels is the speed run, and that is the next thing to build.
  • Every figure is only as good as the turn table, and by Ch 10 only the SS90E pair and the spins have met a floor. The planner will confidently choose a wrong route if the table lies to it, so the arcs want measuring, not eyeballing — entry speed, exit speed and duration, per turn.
  • The planner and turn_smooth() still disagree about chained arcs. At R = v/ω ≈ 101 mm two 90s one cell apart need 202 mm and have 180, so the planner refuses and emits stop-and-spin — while the firmware relabels the frame past the next turn point and simply runs the arcs back to back. Zigzag test (Calibration, or Z) drives that case deliberately. Whichever way it comes out, one of the two is wrong, and the diagonal figures move with it.
  • The goal run-out is assumed at 270 mm. It depends on which cell of the goal box she enters and from where; a tighter entry would cap her lower.
  • Exploration is measured but not yet driven. 0.23 answers how much is left — the optimistic bound and the unseen cells on it — and that is the stopping rule already in hand: explore until the optimistic route crosses no unseen cell, at which point the two times have converged by construction. What is missing is the second objective that acts on it: flood to the nearest useful unseen cell rather than to the goal, drive there, repeat. maze.cell_is_visited() gives the target set for free; act_resume_saved is still a stub.
  • Her explored map may hold only one route. Plan route uses MASK_CLOSED, so after a single explore the world she can plan in is the corridor she drove and nothing else — and all three objectives must then return the same thing, because there is nothing else to return. That is not a flat cost model, it is an empty choice, and it is why Resume matters before any of these figures mean much on the real mouse.
